# R2 — Standalone descriptive evidence pack

**Audit snapshot:** 2026-10-24. **Owner:** issue #9. **Scope:** US national primary-source measures, with an explicit matched 2024 Washington-state comparison and geographic follow-up plan. **Not** a causal account, an integrated affordability index, a Claim Ledger, or an assertion of one official middle-class definition.

## Research discipline

This pack reports distinct observational units (worker, earner, family, Census household, BLS consumer unit, occupied housing unit, student/institution, SHED adult respondent) and distinct estimands (median, mean, share, national-account total, hypothetical response, model output). Reference years are those of the underlying observation, not necessarily the publication year. No year is designated a universal breakpoint.

Evidence status **reviewed** means that a primary publisher table or report was checked and selected numeric observations were transcribed. It does **not** mean microdata were independently reweighted or every history tab extracted. **Identified** means an original source was located but relevant longitudinal estimates have not been transcribed. [Inventory](source-inventory.csv), [observations](data/observations.csv), [definitions](definitions-methods.md), [reproducible calculations](exhibits.md), [measurement log](measurement-uncertainty.md). Individual source IDs S01–S21 reference the CSV, with direct URLs and each measure's population, vintage and disallowed comparisons.

## 2. Housing: four independent analytical lenses

### A. House-price exposure

The Census/HUD [new-home median series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/MSPUS) provides quarterly selling prices for **new** single-family houses sold; the Census CPS income series measures **all households**, renters included, in the calendar year. The illustrative benchmark 1984-Q1/1984 and 2025-Q1/2025 ratios (E1) use no unrecorded interpolation or choice of 1973. A change in size, geographic mix, construction quality, or share of buyers cannot be separated from a repeat-home price change with these two aggregates. Median sale price does not tell us the downpayment, mortgage principal, closing costs, loan rate, tax/maintenance expense, credit standards, owner tenure or interest deductibility.

Mortgage-payment history can be examined using [Freddie Mac PMMS / FRED MORTGAGE30US](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/MORTGAGE30US), but that weekly quoted mortgage-rate population is not a direct cost for a particular household. A cash-flow model needs paired *place, purchase price, deposit, rate, term, property taxes, insurance, maintenance and buyer earnings*; a nationally median-priced newly built home is not necessarily bought by a nationally median-income household. Rates changed survey methodology in 2022; see S06 and the measurement register.

### B. Rent and observed burden

ACS B25064 2024 median **gross** rent ($1,487) refers to **cash-rent-paying renter units**; ACS B19013 income ($81,604) refers to **all households**. Their 21.9% juxtaposition is shown explicitly as a **non-estimate** of renter cost burden, retained because it demonstrates why a plausible calculation can be statistically wrong. Do not quote it as the household's housing percentage.

Use the renter-matched [ACS B25070 detailed table](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2024.B25070). For the United States, total renter-occupied units **46,101,640**, category “not computed” **3,147,163**, so denominator with calculable rent-to-income ratio **42,954,477**. The sum of bins 30–34.9 (**4,056,945**), 35–39.9 (**2,966,608**), 40–49.9 (**4,012,728**), and ≥50% (**11,200,981**) is **22,237,262**. Thus **51.8%** of units *for which burden can be computed* spend **at least 30%** of income on gross rent, and **26.1%** spend at least 50%. The >=30 boundary follows Census bins; a strict “>30%” point threshold would require a different microdata calculation. Cell-specific 90% ACS MOEs, available in the underlying table and inventory, **do not** produce a derived-ratio CI by naïve addition. These values are not the median renter burden.

Washington state, computed identically from the *same* 2024 ACS 1-year table: total **1,175,739**, not computed **56,522**, denominator **1,119,217**, ≥30% count **567,874**, **50.7%**; ≥50% **23.8%**. It is one geographic counterexample to a universal national percentage, not a ranking nor independent evidence of change over time. Additional state/metro comparisons require the same ACS release and MOE review. 2024 ACS 1-year estimates do not cover very small geographies; 5-year ACS is a separate estimand.

### C. Owner occupancy, mortgage, stock versus flow

An owner’s observed monthly housing cost includes mortgage principal and interest (if any), property tax, utilities, insurance, condo fees and maintenance depending on the table. Home purchase price is a **stock acquisition price**; ongoing rental expense and mortgage debt service are **flows**. Owner *with mortgage* versus owner *without mortgage* matters. ACS [B25091](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2024.B25091) and [B25106](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2024.B25106) are identified as more suitable matched cost-burden categories, but no owner burden from those tables has been independently extracted for this pack. Comparing a house price to annual income or aggregate spending is not a substitute.

### D. Geography and selection

A national price can conceal high-cost coastal metros, cheaper areas, jobs in a different commuting basin, and neighborhood-specific housing constraints. The two scenarios labelled low-cost/high-cost intentionally change *assumed* housing and care amounts, **without claiming to represent particular states**. The ACS table permits state-level and metro-level stratification; report each numerator, denominator and MOE separately. Current home/rent mix need not match that of historical household cohorts. No renter/owner selection correction is estimated here.

## 3. Healthcare: separate total resources, payroll contributions and point-of-care payments

The [2026 KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2026-employer-health-benefits-survey/) covers employer-sponsored insurance offered by sampled private and nonfederal public firms with **10 or more workers** and surveyed 1,937 firms. The **annual mean family premium** is **$28,303**, of which the average **covered worker contribution** is **$7,471**. For 2025, KFF reported **$26,993** total and **$6,850** worker contribution. The historical [2008 KFF survey Exhibit 6.4](https://www.kff.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/7790.pdf) lists **1999** total **$5,791**, worker **$1,543**.

The worker's **share** of total mean family premium is about **26.6% (1999)** and **26.4% (2026)**. This ratio cannot show whether the *dollar* worker contribution has become more/less affordable because the series is nominal and no same-worker wage series was paired. Historical survey coverage and sampled employer-firm minimum changed; it is not a clean constant-population premium index. Moreover KFF means are not necessarily the median paid by the same family types across years. Employee contributions vary by company size, geography, plan and network. Averages also omit the value of medical care and out-of-pocket deductibles, co-pays, coinsurance, unpaid bills and non-employer coverage. Those components must not be implicitly set to zero in interpreting healthcare expenses.

[CMS NHE](https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/historical) shows health spending around **18.0% of GDP in 2024**: this is a national-payer definition that includes Medicare, Medicaid and other payer sources. It is not a household's direct expense. Separately, the [2024 BLS CE](https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/consumer-expenditures/2024/home.htm) mean CU health expenditures of **$6,197** are spending based on CE definitions and coverage. Adding that amount to the *full* KFF $28,303 premium for one “average family” would double-count and combine non-matched populations. For stress tests only the KFF **employee contribution** appears as an illustrative cash expense. Other OOP costs are included (unspecified) in synthetic “other essentials”; alternative direct OOP figures can be substituted after extraction. Job loss may change employer coverage; the present job-loss test holds premium constant and flags this as a material limitation.

## 4. Childcare: payer populations, non-payers, and time

The Census [SIPP time series](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/archives/2013-pr/cb13-62.html) reports mean childcare expenditures of **$84/week in 1985** and **$143/week in 2011**, both **2011 dollars**, for families with an employed mother and children under 15 in the report's stated care-cost population. +70.2% is therefore **a within-series real average**, not the percentage increase in the current price of a preschool slot. Changing child age, provider type, subsidization, payer selection and employment matter. In the same Census discussion, the share of families paying cash for care declined from 42% in 1997 to 32% in 2011; conditioning on payments can change a mean independently of provider prices. The 2014 SIPP redesign prevents a naïve extension of pre-2013 series to today.

A household with two wage earners may pay childcare that a one-earner household does not. Conversely, **zero paid childcare** in our single-earner scenario is *not* zero economic time/resource cost. A one-earner household might still use part-time childcare; a dual-earner household might receive unpaid relative care, subsidized care, or school-day coverage. Both are separate sensitivity questions, and neither is resolved by the historical paid-payer mean.

[BLS ATUS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/atus_06252026.htm) indicates adults living with children younger than six spend on average **2.3 hours/day** doing primary childcare (2025). Diaries are person-level and “primary” activities are different from supervision while doing another activity. The 2020 annual collection interruption and sampling changes limit continuous-year and subgroup claims. This pack does not monetize unpaid hours or infer total couple time from adult averages.

## 5. Education: tuition, net price, attendance and borrowing

[NCES Digest 2024 Table 330.10](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d24/tables/dt24_330.10.asp) supplies a long historical degree-granting institution tuition/fees/room-board series reaching back to **1963–64**, with college-type classifications and enrollment weights. For a recent definition alternative, the independently indexed [IPEDS table](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/search/viewtable?tableId=36558) lists public four-year *in-state* mean tuition/fees of **$10,209 in 2014–15** and **$9,016 in 2023–24**, both **constant 2023 dollars** (−11.7%). This selected real institutional-charge measure counters an unqualified assertion that tuition rises in every era under every school/residency definition.

Neither **sticker tuition** nor average “tuition and fees plus room/board” is necessarily the **net price after grant aid**. Students may have different sticker fees, enrollment intensity, residency, housing arrangements or eligibility for grants. A meaningful fixed-household education burden requires observed parent/student household income and net price, separated from *student* debt balances and loan payments. The [Federal Student Aid administrative portfolio](https://studentaid.gov/data-center/student/portfolio) provides federal loan statistics, but private loans, original loan vintage, undergraduate versus graduate borrowing, repayment plan, interest accrued and borrowers versus students matter. No constructed time trend in median graduate debt appears here; it remains identified rather than verified. An aggregate balance is not a median student liability.

## 6. Consumer budgets: spending share is not income share

[BLS CE 2024](https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/consumer-expenditures/2024/home.htm) gives mean annual expenditures across **consumer units** of **$78,535**, pretax CU income **$104,207**, and average 2.4 people/CU. CE consumer units are defined by shared expenditures, not precisely Census all households. 2024 housing = **33.4% of total mean spending** (2021: 33.8%; 2023: 33.0%). Transportation = 17.0% (2021: 16.4%); food = 12.9% (2021: 12.4%); healthcare = 7.9% (2021: 8.1%); education = 2.0% of all CU spending in 2024. E2 reproduces selected changes in **percentage points**, not percent growth. That a category's *share* falls does not prove its nominal spending or inflation-adjusted burden fell; the share depends on all other category spending too.

CE expenses also include personal insurance/pensions distinct from medical insurance; some household “mandatory” contributions differ from modeling concepts. Tax, interest, saving and debt service can be represented differently from household cash ledger construction. Comparing $78,535 mean spending directly with $81,604 ACS median HH income or $87,460 2025 CPS median is an invalid cross-survey proxy for slack. The [BLS historical CE tables](https://www.bls.gov/cex/tables.htm) permit more extensive 1984+ annual mean and consumer-unit-earner income cells, but historical weighting, redesign and category definitions must be validated before a pooled longer series is approved.

“Fixed versus variable” is a **household decision horizon**, not an invariant product label. Within one year a lease, debt contract, insurance contribution and formal childcare reservation are relatively committed; over five years even housing location, mortgage status, childcare arrangements and transport can adjust. Similarly food has an essential minimum but considerable variable component. The model puts rent, specified paid childcare, premiums and debt in fixed one-year buckets for transparency; its synthetic other essentials are conservatively held constant under a 3-month earnings loss. This is a choice, not measured elasticity.

## 7. Household composition and number of earners

The [Census historical household tabulations](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/families-and-living-arrangements.html) document married-couple households declining as a fraction of **all households** from 66% (1975) to 47% (2025); one-person households rising from 20% to 29%. These describe **composition of the total household population**, not necessarily individual family wellbeing. Elderly householders, cohabitants, unrelated roommates, migration, retirement, student living arrangements and adult-child moves change who is in that population.

The number of **earners** is distinct from whether adults are currently **workers**, whether both work full-time, whether there are **children**, and the household unit. A 2025 nationwide *household* median already incorporates differing numbers of workers and family compositions. It cannot be substituted for “the typical worker's salary” or for a fixed two-adult one-child household. For any final time-series claim about middle-income family viability, use composition-adjusted strata of number of adults, child ages, earners, hours and tenure; track how sample weights and strata change. Relevant future primary sources include [CPS ASEC detailed income tables](https://www.census.gov/topics/income-poverty/income/data/tables.html), [ACS PUMS](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs/microdata.html), and [CE public use microdata](https://www.bls.gov/cex/pumd_data.htm), but matched-stratum reproductions are **not** claimed here.

## 8. Resilience and financial shocks

The Fed's [2025 SHED](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-accessible-version.htm) has a $400 hypothetical expense question. **63%** of adult respondents would pay the expense using cash, savings or a credit card paid off at the next statement; this compares to **50%** in 2013 and **68%** in 2021 in its published survey history. **Not** using cash-equivalent payment does not automatically mean the respondent is unable to pay by any means. [2025 savings results](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-savings-investments.htm) separately report **55%** of adults have rainy-day savings to cover three months of expenses and **30%** could not cover three months by any means measured in the question. Both are self-reports of *adults*, not precise assets or proportions of illiquid households; cohorts and question framing matter. Inflation changes what $400 buys over time, a further constraint on fixed-nominal-threshold comparisons.

Our stress model deliberately tests one-time **$6,000** and **$15,000** shocks against a constructed cash balance and a hypothetical 3-month loss of earnings for each earner. These are **chosen event sizes**, not probabilities or distributions of real shocks. No disability, health-plan loss, credit-card interest rate, unemployment insurance, withdrawal restrictions, home equity, liquidity fragmentation or government support is included.

## 9. Model scenarios, sensitivity and limitations

Data input [scenarios.csv](data/scenarios.csv) and exact formula in [definitions-methods.md](definitions-methods.md). All annual wage figures are **hypothetical 2026 USD gross labor income**, not the 2025 household median. Case structure: two adults + one preschooler, annual direct worker contribution of **$7,471** for employer family coverage, annual debt service **$3,000**, starting liquid savings **$10,000**. Synthetic effective income/payroll-tax deductions of 18%, 20%, 22% or 24% are not computed tax liabilities. Monthly “other essentials” include transportation, food, regular health point-of-care costs and other unavoidable expenses not counted elsewhere. Home rent figures, childcare and other essentials are hypothetical.

| Case | Earners/gross wage | Rent/month | Paid childcare/month | Effective tax input | Computed cash margin/month |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| One earner, lower wages | $60k + $0 | $1,600 | $0 | 18% | −$773 |
| Two earners | $60k + $40k | $1,600 | $1,250 | 22% | +$77 |
| One earner, **same gross wages** as dual | $100k + $0 | $1,600 | $0 | 22% | +$1,627 |
| Two earners, lower synthetic cost case | $60k + $40k | $1,200 | $800 | 20% | +$1,394 |
| Two earners, higher synthetic cost case | $60k + $40k | $2,900 | $2,100 | 24% | −$2,439 |

This table is **accounting**, not a finding that one-/two-earner families actually spend these amounts; real behavior includes specialization, unpaid work, benefit eligibility, career effects, childcare subsidies, local rents, tax credits, and selective employment. For same $100k gross wages, the one-/two-earner *constructed* comparison deliberately differs in paid childcare and other essentials. Its result is conditional on those inputs. The high-cost scenario is not named as any metropolitan area. Holding all commitments constant during unemployment is an intentionally restrictive cost-response assumption; short-run childcare and insurance treatment can be rerun, not presumed. The corresponding shortfall calculations in [E3–E4](exhibits.md) reveal sensitivity to assumed liquidity, spending and which earner loses wages.

**Sensitivity parameters and next runs:** repeat wage 1/2 pairs holding total income constant (e.g., 50/50, 60/40, 80/20), childcare $0–$2,500/mo, housing $1,000–$3,500/mo, liquid savings $0/$10k/$25k, loss 1/3/6 months, employer coverage continuing/ending, shock $400/$6k/$15k, taxes via actually computed federal/state filing status, and fixed versus partially adjustable other essentials. These are a specified research grid, **not yet modeled all combinations**; existing five rows already expose price and tax sensitivity, wage concentration and chosen stress sizes. Historical 1984 household expense scenarios would require genuine year-aligned local inputs and historic tax rules; no 1984 salary with a 2026 rent has been presented as observed history.

## 10. Counterevidence and alternatives retained

- Recent IPEDS real in-state *sticker charges fell* over the selected 2014–2023 interval, contrary to a monotonic across-every-definition tuition-cost hypothesis. Net prices and borrower costs remain open.
- Within BLS CE all-consumer-unit spending, aggregate housing **share** declined slightly 2021–2024, despite 2024 growth in nominal housing expenditures. Neither invalidates the other; denominators differ.
- KFF worker fraction of total family premium changed little 1999–2026, while nominal worker dollar contributions changed substantially. The ratio is not the share of household wages.
- Median CPS household income increased **41.3% in its published inflation-adjusted series** between 1984 and 2025; this neither establishes representative individual wage growth nor fixes the household population's composition.
- SHED cash-equivalent response share is higher in 2025 than 2013 but below 2021; no universal monotonically worsening liquidity trend is implied.
- Tenant burden can exceed a simple ratio of national medians because one measure is based on matched **renter** income rather than the income distribution of **all households**; the unpaired ratio must not be promoted to a factual burden measure.
- Childcare spending estimates among *paying* families coexist with declining *cash-paying participation* in the selected historical period. Neither estimates all-family care cost in isolation.

## 11. Unreviewed but needed before claim certification

1. **Full historical aligned affordability series:** matched state/metro ACS renter and owner burden (B25070/B25091/B25106); 1-year versus 5-year panel limits; ACS microdata age, tenure and family structure; full Census decennial historical renter burden definitions.
2. **Owner finance:** existing-home prices, repeat-sale index, PMI/downpayments, property tax/insurance, mortgage rates and loan vintage; actual income of buyers.
3. **Healthcare:** current and historical household-based worker contribution, OOP, deductible, uninsured and public-program strata; harmonization of KFF eligibility changes; CMS/CE scope concordance.
4. **Childcare:** 2014 redesigned SIPP and subsequent prices/arrangements; childcare provider rates by child age and metro, subsidy coverage, unpaid care diaries with time allocation; explicitly nonspliced pre-/post-redesign bridge.
5. **Education:** longer real tuition series by institution/residency, financial aid/net price, debt repayment burden by borrower and cohort (not only portfolio total).
6. **Budget historical segmentation:** CE 1984+ means, income/earners/quintile cross-tabs, documented category and survey redesigns, individual household spending share distribution and income after taxes.
7. **Shocks:** liquid wealth matched by households and demographic groups, Fed SHED question changes, SCF wealth/balance-sheet alternative, uncertainty intervals; unemployment rates and durations by family type.
8. **Composition:** family/household/adult earners and equivalized posttax income fixed-composition panels; refusal to substitute CPS median for worker earnings.

**Completion state:** all major *category families* have original-source identification and examples; **not every relevant primary series has yet been exhaustively extracted, geographically panelled or independently replicated from microdata**. Do not characterize this source-verified evidence pack as complete statistical harmonization. The audit limitations and disallowed comparisons are central research outputs, not missing-data estimates.
